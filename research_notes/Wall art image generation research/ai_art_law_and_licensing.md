# AI Art Law and Licensing for a UK Wall-Art Seller on eBay UK

**Research date: 7 October 2026.** All "current status" statements are as at this date. This area moves fast; the UK government's own position changed materially in March 2026 and the Getty appeal is live.

**Scope note:** The seller is UK-based, generating images at scale and selling physical wall art (prints, canvases, framed posters) on eBay UK. UK law governs. US law is flagged explicitly wherever cited.

## Which image-generation models permit COMMERCIAL use of their output, under what licence?

### Takeaway
Only a handful of art-capable open models are cleanly permissive: **FLUX.1 [schnell], Qwen-Image, segmind SSD-1B and DeepSeek Janus-Pro** are Apache-2.0 or MIT and safe for commercial use of both model and output. The critical and widely-misunderstood case is **FLUX.1 [dev]**: the *model* is non-commercial, but Black Forest Labs' own model card states the *outputs* may be used commercially — the restriction bites on running the model in a business, not on selling the images. Stability's SDXL/SD3.5 family sits under revenue-capped or RAIL-restricted licences, not true open-source.

### Cited Findings

**Apache-2.0 / MIT — cleanly permissive (model and output)**
- **FLUX.1 [schnell]** is released under the Apache 2.0 licence, which permits commercial use, "making it suitable for integration into commercial products, applications, and services. This is one of its key advantages over the Dev variant" — [Morphic AI glossary, FLUX.1 Schnell](https://morphic.com/id/ai-glossary/FLUX1-Schnell)
- **Qwen-Image** (20B parameters): "Qwen-Image is licensed under Apache 2.0" — [Hugging Face, Qwen/Qwen-Image](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen-Image)
- **CAUTION — Qwen-Image 2.1 is different.** "Qwen-Image-2.1 is released under the Qwen Research License Agreement, and you may not use the model 'for any commercial purpose without obtaining a separate commercial license'" — [apidog, What is Qwen-Image-2.1](https://apidog.com/blog/what-is-qwen-image-2-1/); [awesome-qwen-image](https://github.com/wildminder/awesome-qwen-image). So the version number matters: Qwen-Image = Apache-2.0; Qwen-Image-2.1 = research licence.
- **segmind SSD-1B**: model card states "License: apache-2.0", with no commercial restrictions noted — [Hugging Face, segmind/SSD-1B](https://huggingface.co/segmind/SSD-1B). (A 50%-smaller, 60%-faster distilled SDXL.)
- **DeepSeek Janus-Pro** (1B–7B): "Janus-Pro is under an MIT license, meaning it can be used commercially without restriction" — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/27/viral-ai-company-deepseek-releases-new-image-model-family); [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250128-deepseek-janus-pro-image-model). Practical limit: "most Janus-Pro models can only analyze small images with a resolution of up to 384 x 384" — same TechCrunch piece. That resolution ceiling makes Janus unsuitable for print-resolution wall art.

**FLUX.1 [dev] — the model/output split**
- The model is released under the **"FLUX.1 [dev] Non-Commercial License"** (also styled the FluxDev Non-Commercial License Agreement) — [Hugging Face, black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev](https://huggingface.co/black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev)
- The same model card states that generated outputs "can be used for personal, scientific, and commercial purposes as described in the FLUX.1 [dev] Non-Commercial License" — [Hugging Face, black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev](https://huggingface.co/black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev)
- Commentary resolving the apparent contradiction: "while the model is restricted to non-commercial use, the outputs generated by the model can be used commercially, with the exception that outputs cannot be used to train competitive models" — [artificialguy, FLUX Licensing in Plain Terms](https://artificialguy.com/blog/flux-licensing-commercial-use/); [LicenseOrg FLUX guide](https://www.licenseorg.com/guide/ai-content/flux). The licence text itself is sufficiently ambiguous that there is a long-running clarification thread on the model repo — [HF discussion #136](https://huggingface.co/black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev/discussions/136).
- Black Forest Labs introduced **"Self-Hosted Commercial License Terms," last revised 26 June 2025**; a paid BFL commercial licence is required for business use of the model itself — [artificialguy](https://artificialguy.com/blog/flux-licensing-commercial-use/); [DeepWiki, FLUX commercial usage and licensing](https://deepwiki.com/black-forest-labs/flux/5-commercial-usage-and-licensing)
- **FLUX.1 [pro]** is not open-weight: available only via commercial API with usage-based pricing, "optimised for commercial production use" — [Morphic AI glossary, FLUX.1 Dev](https://morphic.com/es/ai-glossary/FLUX1-Dev)

**Stability AI family — revenue-capped Community Licence / RAIL**
- Stability's **Community License** covers "Stable Audio 3.0, Stable Diffusion 3.5 Suite, SDXL Turbo, Stable Fast 3D" plus other core models. Free commercial use for individuals and organisations generating **under USD $1M annually in revenue, "regardless of the source of that revenue."** Above that, "purchase an Enterprise License" — [stability.ai/license](https://stability.ai/license)
- Corroboration on the threshold: the Community Licence "is designed for researchers, developers, small businesses, and creators with less than $1M in annual revenue" — [promptzone, SD3.5 variants and licensing](https://www.promptzone.com/zuri_wang/stable-diffusion-35-variants-licensing-and-prompting-1e15.md); [landian.news](https://landian.news/article/2496.html)
- **CONFLICT ON SDXL-TURBO.** Secondary sources say "SDXL-Turbo is limited by Stability AI's Non-Commercial license, meaning it cannot be used for commercial purposes without obtaining a separate license from Stability AI" — [HF sdxl-turbo README, older revision](https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/sdxl-turbo/blob/2c4b5b9e8c65b03b4cd101620ca468508e51f677/README.md). But Stability's **current** licence page lists SDXL Turbo *inside* the Community License — [stability.ai/license](https://stability.ai/license). Reading: SDXL-Turbo launched (Nov 2023) under the Stability AI Non-Commercial Research Community Licence and was later brought under the Community Licence. The commercially-safe reading today is Community Licence with the $1M cap, but this must be re-verified against the live model card before relying on it.
- **SDXL base 1.0** is under the **CreativeML Open RAIL++-M License** — [Hugging Face, stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0](https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0). RAIL permits commercial use but attaches *use-based restrictions* (the Attachment A prohibited-use list) which flow down to derivatives and must be passed on to downstream recipients.
- **PixArt** (PixArt-XL-2-1024-MS) is likewise under the **CreativeML Open RAIL++-M License** — [Hugging Face, PixArt-alpha/PixArt-XL-2-1024-MS](https://huggingface.co/PixArt-alpha/PixArt-XL-2-1024-MS). An earlier search result also characterised PixArt models as "openrail++, which generally permits commercial use with certain conditions."
- **Playground v2.5** is under the **"Playground v2.5 Community License"** — [Hugging Face, playgroundai/playground-v2.5-1024px-aesthetic](https://huggingface.co/playgroundai/playground-v2.5-1024px-aesthetic). The model card does not state commercial terms on its face; the arXiv paper describes it as released under a licence that "makes it easy for research teams to use" — [arXiv 2402.17245](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17245v1). **Treat as unresolved** — the full licence document must be read before commercial reliance.

### Inferences
- The CLAUDE.md shared fact that "FLUX.1-dev is non-commercial and must never be used on goods that are sold" is **over-broad as to outputs but correct as to the model**. The operative risk is not that a FLUX-dev-generated poster is unsaleable; it is that running FLUX.1 [dev] on RunPod as part of a revenue-generating pipeline is itself the commercial use the licence forbids without a paid BFL licence. For a seller who wants one bright line and no licence negotiation, "schnell only" remains the right operational rule even though the legal reasoning behind it should be restated.
- A revenue-capped licence (SD 3.5, SDXL-Turbo under the Community Licence) is a latent liability for a scaling seller: the licence silently converts from free to requiring an Enterprise Licence at $1M turnover, and the cap is on *all* revenue "regardless of the source."
- RAIL++ is not equivalent to Apache-2.0. Its use restrictions must be reproduced in any downstream distribution of the model or derivative weights. That matters if LoRAs or fine-tunes are ever shared, not for selling prints.

The model/output split rests on the model-card wording plus two secondary commentaries, not on a quoted licence clause. **Recommend the seller or a later session read the licence text directly before relying on commercial use of dev outputs.**
- Playground v2.5 Community License terms not obtained.
- Live SDXL-Turbo model-card licence tag not confirmed on current revision (only an older pinned revision was reachable).
- No source found on whether Apache-2.0 model publishers make any claim to output copyright (they generally do not, but this was not verified for each).

## Under UK law, who owns copyright in an AI-generated image, and can it be protected at all?

### Takeaway
UK law is unusual in having an express computer-generated works provision — **CDPA 1988 s.9(3)**, which assigns authorship to "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken." It is still on the statute book, but the government has signalled it may be repealed, and it is in tension with the EU-derived originality test, so a bulk AI-generated catalogue should be assumed to have **weak or no enforceable copyright**.

### Cited Findings
- **The provision, verbatim:** s.9(3) CDPA 1988 states "In the case of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work which is computer-generated, the author shall be taken to be the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken." — [regulations.ai, Computer-Generated Works glossary](https://regulations.ai/glossary/computer-generated-works-cgws); [recordinglaw.com, UK AI copyright laws](https://www.recordinglaw.com/world-laws/world-ai-copyright-laws/united-kingdom-ai-copyright-laws/)
- **Term:** "The UK's CDPA s.9(3) can give AI-generated works 50-year copyright, but the originality standard and a stalled TDM reform leave the law unsettled in 2026." — [recordinglaw.com](https://www.recordinglaw.com/world-laws/world-ai-copyright-laws/united-kingdom-ai-copyright-laws/) (Note: s.12(7) CDPA gives computer-generated works 50 years from creation, not life+70.)
- **Consultation outcome on s.9(3):** "Most people who responded to the consultation considered that works created solely by AI should not be protected and supported the removal of the protection under s 9(3) CDPA, while retaining protection for AI-assisted works." The government "proposed that in the absence of evidence of its ongoing value, this specific type of protection for wholly computer-generated works should be removed, while copyright should continue to protect works created with AI assistance." — search synthesis of [Lexology, AI & IP consultation: focus on copyright for computer-generated works](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=5506d0f9-98bc-4ec7-a6d0-726d02dd57af) and [Reed Smith, UK consultation on copyright and AI](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/uk-consultation-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/)
- **2026 position:** a March 2026 law-firm note is titled "UK Government Report on Copyright and AI concludes more evidence is needed although **s9(3) CDPA could go**" — [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/ip/2026-03/uk-government-report-on-copyright-and-ai-concludes-more-evidence-is-needed-although-s-9-3-cdpa-could-go) *(headline only — the page body returned HTTP 403 and could not be read)*
- **The originality tension.** The Court of Appeal in *THJ Systems Ltd v Sheridan* (November 2023) restated the UK originality test: "What is required is that the author was able to express their creative abilities in the production of the work by making free and creative choices so as to stamp the work created with their personal touch," and "this criterion is not satisfied where the content of the work is dictated by technical considerations, rules or other constraints which leave no room for creative freedom." — [Creative Commons, UK court clears path for open culture](https://creativecommons.org/2024/01/18/uk-court-clears-path-for-open-culture-to-flourish/); [Institute of Art and Law](https://ial.uk.com/important-copyright-originality/)
- **Academic treatment** of the unresolved interaction between s.9(3) and the originality requirement: [Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice, "Dealing with AI-generated works: lessons from the CDPA section 9(3)"](https://academic.oup.com/jiplp/article/19/1/43/7485196)
- **US contrast (does NOT bind a UK seller):** the US Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v Perlmutter* on 2 March 2026, leaving standing the holding that AI-generated works without human authorship lack copyright protection — "human authorship is a bedrock requirement." — [Norton Rose Fulbright, AI in litigation series: update on AI copyright cases in 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)

### Inferences
- s.9(3) remains in force today, so there is a statutory route by which the person who made "the arrangements necessary" — the seller running the pipeline, not the model publisher — would be the author. But two things undercut relying on it commercially: the government has said it should probably go, and *THJ v Sheridan*'s originality test is hard to satisfy for an image whose content was produced by the model rather than by human creative choice.
- **Practical consequence:** the seller should plan a business that does not depend on being able to stop others copying its images. Bulk AI output is best treated as unprotectable. Where protection matters (a hero design, a brand), add substantial human creative input — composition, overpainting, typography, curation of a specific arrangement — and document it, so the claim rests on normal authorship rather than on s.9(3).
- A UK seller is **not** affected by *Thaler*. That case concerns registration with the US Copyright Office, which has no UK analogue; UK copyright arises automatically without registration.

### Gaps
- Could not read the body of the March 2026 government report or the ministerial statement (HCWS1416 returned 403; two law-firm analyses returned 402/403). The s.9(3) repeal signal therefore rests on a headline plus consultation-stage reporting. **No source confirms s.9(3) has actually been repealed, and nothing suggests it has.** Treat it as in force but under review.
- No UK case found applying s.9(3) to a generative-AI image. The provision has been judicially considered only in very limited contexts (historically a lottery/game-draw case), so there is no authority on who the "arrangements" person is in a prompt-and-model pipeline.

## Live legal risks: training-data litigation and what it means for a downstream seller

### Takeaway
The single most important development for a UK seller is **Getty Images v Stability AI [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), 4 November 2025**, in which the High Court held that **model weights are not an "infringing copy" because they do not store copies of training images**. That reasoning substantially protects downstream users of model outputs. The residual risk is not in the training data but in *outputs that reproduce a recognisable protected sign or image* — which is exactly where Getty won its only (narrow) victory, on trade marks.

### Cited Findings

**Getty v Stability (UK) — settled law for now, subject to appeal**
- Citation and date: *Getty Images (US) Inc & Ors v Stability AI Limited* **[2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch)**, decided **4 November 2025** by **Joanna Smith J** — [Browne Jacobson case analysis](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/getty-images-stability-ai-judgment-uk-copyright-law-analysis)
- "This was the first UK judgment addressing copyright infringement issues arising from the use and training of generative AI models." — [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/uk/stability-ai-defeats-getty-images-copyright-claims-in-first-of-its-kind-dispute-before-the-high-cour)
- **Training claim abandoned:** "Getty Images abandoned its copyright infringement claim regarding the training and development process after conceding no evidence showed training occurred in the UK." — [Browne Jacobson](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/getty-images-stability-ai-judgment-uk-copyright-law-analysis). Stability's defence was that "development and training of Stable Diffusion occurred outside the UK" — [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/uk/stability-ai-defeats-getty-images-copyright-claims-in-first-of-its-kind-dispute-before-the-high-cour)
- **Secondary infringement failed — the key holding.** "the judge ruled that while AI model weights could theoretically be 'articles,' they were not 'infringing copies' because model weights don't store copies of training images — they represent learned patterns instead." The judge: "the model weights were not themselves an infringing copy of Getty Images' copyright works." — [Browne Jacobson](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/getty-images-stability-ai-judgment-uk-copyright-law-analysis)
- The High Court "rejected the central copyright allegation and found only limited liability for trade mark infringement" — [Cleary IP Tech Insights](https://www.clearyiptechinsights.com/2025/11/uk-high-court-issues-landmark-ruling-in-getty-images-v-stability-ai-with-narrow-trademark-infringement-win-for-getty-claim-of-secondary-copyright-infringement-fails/)
- **Trade mark findings (the narrow Getty win):** iStock watermark — double-identity and likelihood-of-confusion infringement found for Stable Diffusion versions 1.x through 1.4; Getty watermark — infringement limited to specific model versions on likelihood of confusion only; **s.10(3) (dilution/unfair advantage) rejected entirely.** The judge stressed the findings were "highly fact sensitive" and applied only to the particular watermark examples in evidence. — [Browne Jacobson](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/getty-images-stability-ai-judgment-uk-copyright-law-analysis)
- **Appeal pending:** an appeal judgment in *Getty v Stability AI* is anticipated during 2026 — [Hogan Lovells / HLC, AI and copyright: UK outlook for 2026](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/ai-and-copyright-uk-outlook-for-2026)
- **What was left undecided for output liability:** because Getty dropped the claim that making the model available in the UK infringed, there is no definitive UK guidance on whether AI outputs infringe the reproduction or communication-to-the-public rights — "the claimant dropped this claim at trial" — [DLA Piper, Can AI-generated content infringe pre-existing copyright works? (Q2 2026)](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/04/ai-and-copyright-major-issues-and-direction-of-travel-in-the-uk-eu-and-china-in-q2-2026/can-ai-generated-content-infringe-pre-existing-copyright-works)
- **Liability allocation unresolved:** "we are not aware of any cases in the UK...confirming the liability of a user" for intentional creation or distribution of infringing outputs; "Primary liability would typically fall on AI model providers, though contractual indemnities may shift exposure." — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/04/ai-and-copyright-major-issues-and-direction-of-travel-in-the-uk-eu-and-china-in-q2-2026/can-ai-generated-content-infringe-pre-existing-copyright-works)
- **Commentary urging reform notwithstanding the win:** [Wolters Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/getty-images-v-stability-ai-a-landmark-judgment-reinforcing-the-need-for-the-uk-government-to-amend-its-copyright-laws/); further analyses at [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102lvxe/getty-image-loses-copyright-infringement-claim-against-stability-ai-in-uks-first), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/11/getty-images-v-stability-ai-what-the-high-courts-decision-means-for-rights-holders-and-ai-developers), [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/getty-images-v-stability-ai-the-uk-courts-first-word-on-use-of-copyright-works-in-ai-model-development), [Squire Patton Boggs](https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/getty-images-us-inc-and-others-v-stability-ai-limited/), [Clayton Utz](https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2025/november/getty-images-v-stability-ai-implications-for-intellectual-property-data-protection-and-ai-regulation)

**US cases — LABELLED US LAW; none directly binds a UK seller**
- ***Bartz et al. v Anthropic*** (settled): settlement of **USD $1.5 billion**, approx **$3,000 per work**, with destruction of the pirated dataset; final approval given by a federal judge in **July 2026**. Holding: "LLM training on copyrighted works constitutes fair use, but storing pirated copies does not." — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026); [TechCrunch, 20 July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/anthropics-landmark-1-5b-copyright-settlement-is-approved/)
- ***Thomson Reuters v Ross Intelligence***: summary judgment for Thomson Reuters (Feb 2025); copying Westlaw headnotes to train a competing tool was **not** fair use. The Third Circuit affirmed, holding Westlaw's 2,243 headnotes original. — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)
- ***Kadrey et al. v Meta***: partial dismissal June 2025; LLM training qualifies as fair use "regardless of content source legitimacy"; remaining claims concern alleged torrent "seeding" of pirated works. — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)
- ***In Re OpenAI Copyright Infringement*** (MDL, SDNY, consolidated April 2025): discovery ongoing; court ordered production of millions of output logs as of March 2026; no merits decision. — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)
- ***Andersen v Stability AI*** (N.D. Cal., filed 13 Jan 2023) — the visual-artists class action, and the most directly relevant US case for wall art. After Judge Orrick's **12 August 2024** order the surviving claims are: (1) direct copyright infringement against Stability, Midjourney and DeviantArt for use of the artists' works as training data; (2) induced copyright infringement against Stability and Runway for distributing infringing Stable Diffusion models to third parties; and **(3) Lanham Act trade dress and unfair competition claims tied to outputs generated "in the style of" named artists.** Still in discovery; most recent amended complaint February 2026. — [Mesh IP Law litigation tracker](https://www.meshiplaw.com/litigation-tracker/andersen-v-stability-ai); [Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/artificial-intelligence-and-artists-intellectual-property-unpacking-copyright-infringement-allegations-in-andersen-v-stability-ai-ltd/); [Knowing Machines legal explainer](https://knowingmachines.org/knowing-legal-machines/legal-explainer/cases/andersen-v-stability-ai)
- Plaintiffs in *Andersen* pleaded examples of artists' names used as Midjourney prompts producing style-mimicking outputs; "The court found that these examples helped demonstrate the plausibility of the plaintiffs' claim that their works were not only used in the training process but that the resulting AI models retained and reproduced elements of those works." — [Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/artificial-intelligence-and-artists-intellectual-property-unpacking-copyright-infringement-allegations-in-andersen-v-stability-ai-ltd/)
- ***Disney et al. v Midjourney*** (C.D. Cal., pending): alleges unlawful copying for image-generation training **and derivative character reproduction**. — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)
- Direction of travel across the US cases: "the dispute is shifting from whether AI training is lawful to how the training data was obtained and what the training does to the market for the underlying works." — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)

### Inferences
- **For a downstream UK seller, training-data litigation is close to a non-risk.** Every live claim is against model developers for acts of training, scraping or distribution. The *Getty* holding that weights are not infringing copies removes the most obvious theory by which running a model, or possessing its weights, could make a UK seller a secondary infringer.
- The realistic downstream exposure is **output-level**: an output that reproduces a substantial part of a specific protected work, or that carries a recognisable trade mark. Getty's only win was on watermarks appearing in outputs. The transferable lesson is concrete: **reject any generated image containing watermark-like marks, signatures, logos or legible brand text** before it reaches a listing. That is both a copyright and a trade mark control, and it is cheap to automate.
- *Disney v Midjourney* is a US case against a model operator, but it signals that character-IP holders are now actively litigating generative output. For a seller, character and franchise subjects are the highest-risk category regardless of which jurisdiction the case law develops in, because the enforcement route that actually reaches the seller is eBay VeRO, not a court.
- The absence of any UK case confirming user liability for outputs is a gap, not a safe harbour. A seller is a primary infringer in the ordinary way if it *issues copies to the public* of something that reproduces a substantial part of a protected work — distributing a print is an act of primary infringement by the distributor, independent of how the file was made.

### Gaps
- Status of **Getty's US case** against Stability (N.D. Cal. / D. Del.) not established; the Norton Rose Fulbright update explicitly did not cover it. Reports that Getty narrowed or dropped US claims in late 2025 could not be verified from a reliable source.
- No reliable source found on whether *Andersen* has produced any ruling on the merits of the "style" Lanham Act claim (it survived a motion to dismiss, which is a plausibility finding only, not a holding that style imitation is unlawful).
- Whether the *Getty* appeal challenges the "model weights are not an infringing copy" holding specifically is not established.

## What is categorically unsafe to generate and sell, and which right does each engage?

### Takeaway
The unsafe set divides cleanly by legal basis: **copyright** for characters, film/game artwork and specific living artists' works; **trade mark (and criminal offences)** for logos, brand names and royal insignia; **passing off** — not a publicity right — for celebrity likeness in the UK; and **sui generis statutory bars** for royal emblems and protected designations.

### Cited Findings

**Copyright — characters, franchise artwork, specific works**
- "If images, drawings, or photographs of fictional characters are being copied to a substantial extent without licence of the owner, then copyright infringement occurs." — search synthesis of [RPC, The "I dos" and don'ts of Royal marketing and merchandise](https://rpclegal.com/thinking/trainees-take-on-business/the-i-dos-and-donts-of-royal-marketing-and-merchandise) and [Novagraaf, The Royal Wedding: What's the IP etiquette?](https://www.novagraaf.com/en/insights/royal-wedding-whats-ip-etiquette/)
- The UK infringement test is reproduction of a **substantial part** of a specific protected work; the EU formulation (CJEU *Mio/Konektra*) is that "copied original choices must be recognizable in the infringing work" — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/04/ai-and-copyright-major-issues-and-direction-of-travel-in-the-uk-eu-and-china-in-q2-2026/can-ai-generated-content-infringe-pre-existing-copyright-works)
- Character IP is being actively litigated against image generators: *Disney et al. v Midjourney* alleges "derivative character reproduction" — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ce8eaa5f/ai-in-litigation-series-an-update-on-ai-copyright-cases-in-2026)

**Trade mark — logos, brand names, and royal insignia (some criminal)**
- Getty won on trade mark where watermarks appeared in outputs: double-identity and confusion infringement for the iStock watermark in SD 1.x–1.4 — [Browne Jacobson](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/getty-images-stability-ai-judgment-uk-copyright-law-analysis). This is direct authority that a *sign appearing in an AI output*, used on goods, can infringe.
- **Royal insignia.** The Trade Marks Act 1994 prohibits registration of marks denoting a link with the royal family, "including the Royal Arms, images of the crown, royal flags or other emblems, images of any member of the royal family, and any mark likely to suggest royal patronage or authorisation." — [Novagraaf](https://www.novagraaf.com/en/insights/royal-wedding-whats-ip-etiquette/)
- **Criminal offence.** "Under section 99 of the Trade Marks Act it is a criminal offence to use the Royal Arms or deceptively similar designs in a way that leads to the belief that the user is authorised to do so, and it is also an offence to use any device, emblem or title calculated to lead people to believe the user is employed by or supplies goods or services to any member of the Royal Family." — [Novagraaf](https://www.novagraaf.com/en/insights/royal-wedding-whats-ip-etiquette/)
- Royal souvenir rules: manufacturers "must ensure consumers are not misled into believing the Royal Family has endorsed their products; a breach can result in a fine of up to £500," and the Trade Descriptions Act 1968 prohibits false indications of Royal approval with fines up to £400 and possible prison sentences. — [LawFuel](https://www.lawfuel.com/prince-harry-megan-markle-how-to-avoid-an-illegal-royal-affront/); [Osborne Clarke marketinglaw](https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/marketing-techniques/royal-wedding-warning-for-advertisers/)
- **Royal photographs are separately copyright-protected.** "Photographs or artistic renditions will be subject to permission of the copyright owner"; for official royal images copyright is held by Kensington Palace and official guidance stipulates "no commercial use of the official engagement photographs." — [Novagraaf](https://www.novagraaf.com/en/insights/royal-wedding-whats-ip-etiquette/)

**Celebrity likeness — passing off, not a publicity right (UK-specific and important)**
- "the UK does not have a codified legislation that explicitly protects such rights. In English Law there is no 'image right' or 'character right' which allows a celebrity to control the use of his or her name or image; simply because the name or image of a celebrity appears upon a consumable commercial item, it by no means follows that the public will assume that it has been endorsed by that celebrity." — [CMS, Personality rights in the UK](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/personality-rights-in-the-uk-possible-reform-on-the-horizon-to-address-ai-related-challenges)
- "passing off remains the most commonly used cause of action for protecting personality rights in the UK," requiring (i) goodwill or reputation in the name/image/likeness; (ii) misrepresentation such that the public is likely to believe the goods are endorsed by the claimant; (iii) damage. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/personality-rights-in-the-uk-possible-reform-on-the-horizon-to-address-ai-related-challenges)
- Leading cases: *Irvine v Talksport Ltd* [2002] EWHC 367 (Ch) (false endorsement succeeded); *Fenty v Arcadia* — the Court of Appeal dismissed Topshop's appeal, confirming unauthorised use of a Rihanna image on a t-shirt "amounted to passing off." — [RPC, IP Alert: Court of Appeal confirms Rihanna's image protected under the 'umbrella' of passing off](https://www.rpclegal.com/thinking/ip/ip-alert-court-of-appeal-confirms-rihannas-image-protected/); [Briffa, Image Rights – Rihanna](https://briffa.com/blog/image-rights-rihanna)
- Reform is under discussion specifically because of AI: [CMS, "Personality rights in the UK – Possible reform on the horizon to address AI-related challenges"](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/personality-rights-in-the-uk-possible-reform-on-the-horizon-to-address-ai-related-challenges)

### Exclusion list, mapped to the right engaged

| Subject | Right engaged | UK basis | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Film/TV/game/comic characters (Disney, Nintendo, Marvel, Pokémon, anime) | Copyright (artistic work) + trade mark | CDPA substantial-part test; TMA 1994 | **Exclude absolutely** — also the top VeRO trigger |
| Film posters, game key art, album covers | Copyright | CDPA | **Exclude** |
| Logos, brand names, team crests, brand typography | Trade mark | TMA 1994 | **Exclude**; also reject outputs with incidental legible brand text |
| Watermarks / stock-agency marks appearing in output | Trade mark | *Getty v Stability* [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch) | **Exclude** — reject the image, this is a proven infringement route |
| Living named artists' style or works | Copyright if a specific work is reproduced; style alone not protected; possible passing off if endorsement implied | CDPA; passing off | **Exclude** — risk/benefit is bad, see next section |
| Celebrity likeness | Passing off (false endorsement); plus copyright in any source photograph; plus UK GDPR for identifiable living persons | *Irvine*; *Fenty v Arcadia* | **Exclude** |
| Royal family likeness, Royal Arms, crowns, royal emblems | Trade mark registration bar + **criminal offence** under s.99 TMA 1994; copyright in official photographs; Trade Descriptions Act 1968 | TMA 1994 s.99 | **Exclude absolutely** — criminal exposure |
| Protected designations (PDO/PGI, e.g. Champagne, Stilton, Scotch Whisky), Olympic symbols, Red Cross emblem, hallmarks | Sui generis statutory protection | Various | **Exclude** — see Gaps |

### Inferences
- The UK's lack of a publicity right is a **narrow** advantage, not a licence. *Fenty v Arcadia* shows that putting a recognisable celebrity image on a consumer good sold at retail is precisely the fact pattern that succeeds in passing off, because the context implies endorsement. Wall art sold under a shop name is structurally similar to the Topshop t-shirt. Treat celebrity likeness as out.
- Royal subjects deserve a separate, harder rule than other IP because s.99 TMA 1994 creates **criminal** liability, and criminal exposure is not insurable or appealable the way a VeRO takedown is.
- A single automated gate catches a large fraction of this risk: reject any output containing detectable text, logo-like marks or signatures. Outputs with legible text are the ones most likely to carry a mark, and AI text is usually garbled anyway, so the filter costs little.

### Gaps
- No source obtained on PDO/PGI, Olympic Symbols etc. (Protection) Act 1995, or the Royal Arms beyond the TMA s.99 point. These are flagged from general knowledge and **should be verified before being stated as findings in the final report.**
- No UK authority found on whether a text-to-image model reproducing a *character* (as opposed to a specific drawing of it) infringes. UK copyright protects works, not characters as such; this is an open question and should be presented as such.
- UK GDPR / data-protection exposure for AI-generated images resembling identifiable living people was not researched.

## Where is the line on "in the style of"?

### Takeaway
**Style itself is not protected by copyright in the UK or anywhere else** — copyright protects expression of an idea, not the idea or manner. So "in the style of a dead, out-of-copyright artist" is genuinely safe as a matter of copyright, provided no specific surviving work is reproduced. A **named living artist** is a different matter: the copyright risk is low but non-zero (memorised specific works), and the real exposure moves to trade mark, passing off and platform enforcement — with the US *Andersen* Lanham Act claims showing where litigation is heading.

### Cited Findings
- "Style is considered to be somewhat intangible and unfixed and is not typically protected by copyright law... longstanding positive law and commentary hold that copyright does not protect literary or artistic style. This is because copyright law aims to protect the expression of an idea, not the idea itself." — search synthesis of [Mayer Brown / JSM, Drawing the line: copyright issues in AI-generated style imitation](https://www.jsm.com/publications/2025/05/drawing-the-line-copyright-issues-in-ai-generated-style-imitation) and [Cullen & Dykman, AI and Artistic Style Imitation](https://www.cullenllp.com/blog/ai-and-artistic-style-imitation-emerging-copyright-implications/)
- "Generative AI may not breach copyright when it produces artifacts 'in the style of' someone, but it does make use of something that is an economic asset traded within the cultural industries." An AI output "can be merely 'in the style of' a third-party... such that the work 'could have been' produced by that third-party without resembling any of their actual works." — [Taylor & Francis, "In the style of: exploring industry, creator and legal implications of copying style through generative AI"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2496905)
- UK position on alternative routes: "Claimants aggrieved by the use of AI-generated works 'in their style' might look beyond copyright law for legal recourse. Trade mark protection could apply if elements of the style in question are protected as trade marks or are otherwise distinctive." — search synthesis of [Mayer Brown / JSM](https://www.jsm.com/publications/2025/05/drawing-the-line-copyright-issues-in-ai-generated-style-imitation)
- Copyright requires substantial similarity to **specific works**, not stylistic imitation, which makes a "derivative work" theory for style difficult — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/04/ai-and-copyright-major-issues-and-direction-of-travel-in-the-uk-eu-and-china-in-q2-2026/can-ai-generated-content-infringe-pre-existing-copyright-works)
- **US — the live counter-pressure (does not bind UK, but shapes platform behaviour):** in *Andersen v Stability AI* the surviving claims include "Lanham Act trade dress and unfair competition claims tied to outputs generated 'in the style of' named artists" — [Mesh IP Law](https://www.meshiplaw.com/litigation-tracker/andersen-v-stability-ai). Plaintiffs' examples of artist names used as prompts helped establish plausibility that models "retained and reproduced elements of those works" — [Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/artificial-intelligence-and-artists-intellectual-property-unpacking-copyright-infringement-allegations-in-andersen-v-stability-ai-ltd/)
- Further academic treatment: [Columbia Law, AI Image Outputs "in the style of ..."](https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/faculty_scholarship/4544); [RAND, Artificial Intelligence Impacts on Copyright Law](https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PEA3243-1.html)
- Copyright duration framing the "dead artist" question: the basic UK term is **life of the author plus 70 years** for artworks and photographs — [Bridgeman Images, What is copyright and when does it expire](https://www.bridgemanimages.com/en/news/what-is-copyright-and-when-does-it-expire/16802)

### Inferences — the practical line
- **Safe (copyright):** generating in the manner of an artist who died more than 70 years ago — Van Gogh (d. 1890), Monet (d. 1926), Hokusai, Klimt (d. 1918), Mucha (d. 1939), Art Deco and Art Nouveau idioms generally. Style is unprotected and the underlying works are out of copyright, so even a close evocation is defensible. Still check that the output is not a near-copy of one famous *specific* painting, because reproducing a public-domain painting is fine on copyright but may collide with museum/agency image claims and with eBay's own content rules.
- **Grey — avoid naming:** artists who died within the last 70 years (Picasso d. 1973, Dalí d. 1989, Warhol d. 1987, Rothko d. 1970, Hopper d. 1967). Style is still unprotected, but their specific works are in copyright, the model is more likely to have memorised them, and the estates are active enforcers. **Do not use their names in prompts and do not use their names in listing titles or tags.**
- **Avoid entirely — living/recent named artists:** the copyright case is weak but the non-copyright exposure is real (passing off if the listing implies association; trade mark if the name is registered — many contemporary artists register their names), and a listing that says "in the style of [living artist]" is an admission that hands a VeRO reporter everything they need. The commercial upside does not justify it.
- **Crucial operational distinction:** using an artist's name in the **prompt** is a private act with evidential risk only if discoverable; using it in the **listing title, description or tags** is a public act that creates discoverability, supports a false-endorsement argument, and is the single most likely trigger for a takedown. Even where the generation is lawful, **never name a non-public-domain artist in listing metadata.** Describe the aesthetic instead ("1920s Art Deco geometric", "impressionist harbour scene", "mid-century abstract colour field").
- Because style is unprotected, the catalogue's safest strategy is to build its own house aesthetics described generically, rather than borrowing named-artist labels for SEO. This also reduces near-duplication, which the CLAUDE.md records as the main commercial risk.

### Gaps
- No UK case found on style imitation by generative AI. The "style is not protected" proposition is well-established copyright doctrine but was sourced here from law-firm commentary and academic work, not UK authority. The final report should present it as settled doctrine, and the AI application as untested.
- Whether any UK artist has brought a passing-off claim over AI style imitation: no source found; likely none exists.

## eBay's VeRO programme

### Takeaway
VeRO is a **notice-and-takedown programme operated for rights owners, not an adjudication**: a participating rights owner reports a listing, eBay removes it, and the seller's recourse is an internal appeal or persuading the reporter to retract. Consequences escalate from warning to restriction to **account suspension**, so for a high-volume seller the real risk is cumulative, and the mitigation is prevention rather than appeal.

### Cited Findings
- "The Verified Rights Owner programme (VeRO) allows intellectual property rights owners to report listings or items that infringe on their protected rights." — [eBay UK, VeRO programme policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)
- Rights owners participating in VeRO "can create participant profiles explaining their policies on infringing items," which sellers can read to understand specific brand requirements — [eBay UK VeRO policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)
- Grounds for removal cover "copyrights, trademarks, designs, patents, and utility models," including counterfeits, unauthorised copies, trademark misuse, and design/patent violations — [eBay UK VeRO policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)
- **Consequences, quoted:** eBay "may take action consistent with applicable laws and the eBay User Agreement, and may even be legally required to do so. Such actions may include, as an example only: Removing the listing or other content, issuing a warning, restricting activity or account suspension." — [eBay UK VeRO policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)
- **Mitigation per eBay's own guidance:** create original content, use brand names appropriately (adding "fits/for" where applicable), and review VeRO participant profiles before listing — [eBay UK VeRO policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)
- **Appeal:** sellers "may appeal the decision through our internal appeals process" or request retraction from the rights owner directly — [eBay UK VeRO policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/verified-rights-owner-vero-program?id=4349)

### Inferences
- VeRO removal does not require the reporter to prove infringement to eBay's satisfaction first; eBay acts on the notice. This asymmetry means **a seller cannot rely on being legally right** — a defensible "in the style of" listing can still be removed, and the removal still counts against the account. Prevention is the only effective control.
- For a catalogue generated at scale, the exposure is proportional to the number of listings touching enforced subject matter. A single policy — exclude the categories in the exclusion list above, and never name protected subjects in titles/tags — removes most of it.
- Reviewing VeRO participant profiles for the categories being listed (wall art, posters, prints: eBay category 360 per CLAUDE.md) before upload is a cheap, concrete pre-flight step, and eBay explicitly recommends it.
- Keeping the per-account listing footprint separable matters: because suspension is account-level, a seller running multiple accounts should not share listing text or imagery across them in a way that lets one takedown propagate.

### Gaps
- No source found on whether eBay operates a formal "strike" count or threshold before suspension, or how long VeRO marks persist on an account. eBay's published policy states possible actions but not thresholds.
- No source found on whether eBay UK operates any pre-screening of listings for IP (as opposed to reactive VeRO notices).
- The EU Digital Services Act notice-and-action requirements, which affect how marketplaces handle such notices, were not researched.

## Does eBay or any major marketplace require AI-generated content to be disclosed?

### Takeaway
**No** — as of 2026 eBay does not require an "AI-generated" label on listings. The binding rule is **accuracy**: the image must represent the actual item sold. For AI-made wall art that is straightforward, because the AI image *is* the product. The forward-looking item is the **EU AI Act Article 50**, with labelling obligations from **August 2026**, relevant if selling into the EU.

### Cited Findings
- "eBay allows AI editing and AI-generated backdrops, and it ships its own AI background tool in the seller app. However, the rule that binds is accuracy: the image must represent the actual item being sold." — [nightjar.so, Does eBay allow AI-generated product photos in listings?](https://nightjar.so/help-desk/does-ebay-allow-ai-generated-product-photos-in-listings)
- "as of 2026, eBay's policies do not currently require a 'this image was AI-generated' label or tag on individual listings." — [nightjar.so, Are AI generated product images allowed: platform rules](https://nightjar.so/blog/are-ai-generated-product-images-allowed-platform-rules)
- For **used** items the main image must be a real photograph of the actual unit; "stock photos and fully synthetic renders are not allowed as the main image for used goods, except in Books, Movies, Music, and Video Games." — [nightjar.so](https://nightjar.so/help-desk/does-ebay-allow-ai-generated-product-photos-in-listings)
- "sellers shipping into the EU should plan for the EU AI Act's Article 50 obligations, which come into force in August 2026 and require AI-generated content to be labelled in some contexts." — [nightjar.so](https://nightjar.so/blog/are-ai-generated-product-images-allowed-platform-rules)
- eBay has itself been inserting AI-generated model images into seller listings without seller consent — [Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-tests-ai-generated-fashion-model-images-alters-images/); [Shopifreaks](https://www.shopifreaks.com/ebay-is-testing-ai-generated-fashion-model-images-and-inserting-them-into-seller-listings-without-permission/); seller complaints at [eBay community](https://community.ebay.com/t5/Selling/Unwanted-and-Unremovable-AI-Image-added-to-my-Listing-without-my/m-p/35574664?nobounce). Broader transparency criticism at [Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-ai-transparency-concerns/).
- Etsy, by contrast, has AI disclosure/metadata expectations — [aimetadatacleaner, Etsy AI disclosure metadata guide 2026](https://aimetadatacleaner.com/blog/etsy-ai-disclosure-metadata-guide-2026) *(low-quality source: a vendor blog promoting a metadata tool; treat as a pointer only, not authority)*

### Inferences
- Because wall art sold as new, made-to-order prints is listed as **new**, the "real photograph of the actual unit" rule for used goods does not bite. The accuracy rule still does: the listing image must show what the buyer receives, so a mockup must accurately represent print size, crop, framing and border.
- Not being *required* to disclose is different from it being unwise. Describing the item honestly as an AI-generated or digitally-created design is low-cost, reduces misrepresentation and unfair-commercial-practices exposure under UK consumer law, and pre-positions for EU AI Act labelling. **Recommend voluntary, plain disclosure in the description.**
- The EU AI Act Article 50 point matters only for sales into the EU. A UK-only eBay.co.uk operation is outside it, but any cross-border listing brings it in from August 2026.

### Gaps
- Could not reach eBay's own official page on AI-generated images or image requirements; the eBay-specific findings in this section come from a third-party help-desk site (nightjar.so), **not from eBay**. These should be verified against eBay's official image and listing policies before the final report states them as eBay policy.
- No source found on Amazon's or Redbubble's current AI disclosure rules.
- EU AI Act Article 50 scope was not read in primary form; whether it applies to a product image at all (as opposed to deepfakes and synthetic media) is not established and the "in some contexts" hedge in the source is doing real work.

## Public domain: what is reliably safe in the UK, and what traps exist

### Takeaway
UK copyright in artworks and photographs runs for **life of the author plus 70 years**, so pre-1956-ish deaths are reliably clear. The major 2023 development in the seller's favour is **THJ v Sheridan**, which confirms that **a faithful photograph of a 2D public-domain artwork attracts no new copyright** — so museum "image rights" claims over flat reproductions are not copyright claims. Crown copyright runs on different, shorter rules.

### Cited Findings
- "The basic copyright term in the United Kingdom is life of the author plus 70 years, which applies to both visual artworks (such as paintings, drawings, and sculptures) and photographs." — [Bridgeman Images](https://www.bridgemanimages.com/en/news/what-is-copyright-and-when-does-it-expire/16802); [Wikimedia Commons, Copyright rules by territory: United Kingdom](https://c.enwp.org/wiki/Commons:FOP_UK)
- **Crown copyright:** basic term of 50 years from date of commercial publication. For works created before 30 June 1957: Crown photographs 50 years from creation; published Crown engravings 50 years from commercial publication; other Crown artistic works 50 years from creation. — [Wikimedia Commons, UK copyright rules](https://c.enwp.org/wiki/Commons:FOP_UK)
- Guidance on generative AI and Crown copyright for UK government departments exists: [The National Archives, Generative AI and Crown copyright](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ai-crown-copyright.pdf)
- **No new copyright in flat reproductions.** "a November 2023 Appeal Court judgement clarified that no new copyright is created in making a photographic reproduction of a two-dimensional public domain artwork, and that this has been the case since 2009." — [Wikimedia Commons, UK copyright rules](https://c.enwp.org/wiki/Commons:FOP_UK)
- The case is *THJ Systems Ltd v Sheridan* (Court of Appeal, November 2023). The court held the originality test requires that "the author was able to express their creative abilities in the production of the work by making free and creative choices so as to stamp the work created with their personal touch," and that "this criterion is not satisfied where the content of the work is dictated by technical considerations, rules or other constraints which leave no room for creative freedom." — [Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/2024/01/18/uk-court-clears-path-for-open-culture-to-flourish/); [Institute of Art and Law](https://ial.uk.com/important-copyright-originality/)
- Consequence: "copyright cannot be claimed for direct copies of out-of-copyright two-dimensional works of art. This overturned the previous standard under the 1988 Copyright, Designs and Patents Act, which set a low threshold for acquiring copyright. If a photograph of a painting involved some 'skill and labour,' it was protected by copyright." — [Museums Association](https://www.museumsassociation.org/museums-journal/opinion/2024/02/how-does-a-recent-landmark-ruling-change-museums-understanding-of-copyright/); [Artlyst](https://artlyst.com/uk-museums-banned-from-imposing-reproduction-fees-after-70-years/)
- **The trap, flagged by the same sources:** "While institutions may continue to charge for the supply of high-resolution files, low-resolution files of out-of-copyright works are now freely accessible," and "museums might still block it" — [Wikimedia Commons](https://c.enwp.org/wiki/Commons:FOP_UK); [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2024/02/22/)

### Inferences — reliable vs trap
**Reliably safe in the UK:**
- Works by artists who died before roughly 1956 (70 years before 2026). Van Gogh, Monet, Hokusai, Klimt, Mucha, Turner, Vermeer, Japanese ukiyo-e, botanical and ornithological plates, vintage maps and patent drawings of that vintage.
- Generating *new* images in the manner of those traditions: doubly safe, since style is unprotected and the source works are out of copyright.
- The flat-reproduction point means a scan or photo of such a painting carries no fresh copyright, so a public-domain painting used as a reference or input is not laundered back into copyright by the museum that photographed it.

**Traps:**
1. **Contract, not copyright.** Museums and image libraries license high-resolution files under terms of use. *THJ* removes the copyright claim but not the contractual one — downloading a hi-res file under a licence that forbids commercial use creates breach-of-contract and database-right exposure even though no copyright subsists. Use genuinely open sources, or generate rather than reproduce.
2. **Jurisdiction.** Term is life+70 in the UK but differs elsewhere (US rules turn on publication date and the pre-1930 cutoff). An artwork public domain in the UK may not be in the destination country. Only relevant for cross-border sales.
3. **3D objects and modern elements.** The *THJ* reasoning is about **two-dimensional** works. A photograph of a sculpture or building involves creative choices and can be original. Likewise a modern restoration, colourisation or arrangement can carry its own copyright.
4. **Crown copyright's different clock.** 50 years from commercial publication, not life+70, with pre-1957 variants. Do not apply the life+70 rule to government material.
5. **Trade marks never expire.** A public-domain *artwork* can still contain a live trade mark, and the artist's name or signature may be a registered mark even where the works are out of copyright.
6. **Moral rights.** The CDPA right to be identified as author and to object to derogatory treatment runs for the same period as copyright in most cases, so it is not an additional constraint for pre-1956 artists — but it is for the 70-year grey zone.

### Gaps
- No primary citation obtained for *THJ Systems Ltd v Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354 itself; the holding is sourced from Creative Commons, the Museums Association and the Institute of Art and Law. Reliable and consistent across three sources, but the neutral citation should be verified.
- No UK IPO guidance page on copyright duration was reached directly; duration rules come from Bridgeman Images and Wikimedia Commons. Both are consistent with the statutory position but are not official.
- Database right in museum image collections, and the UK position on "sweat of the brow" after *THJ*, not researched.
- The pre-1957 and 1956 Act transitional provisions for artistic works (which can extend or alter terms for some older works) were not researched.

## Cross-cutting practical recommendations

### Takeaway
A defensible UK AI wall-art operation comes down to four decisions: run an Apache-2.0 model, exclude a fixed list of subjects, filter outputs for marks and text, and never name protected subjects in listing metadata.

### Inferences
1. **Model:** run **FLUX.1 [schnell]** (Apache-2.0) as the default. It is the only high-quality model that is unambiguously clear on both model and output, with no revenue cap and no RAIL restrictions. **Qwen-Image** (Apache-2.0, 20B) is the strongest alternative — but Qwen-Image**-2.1** is not, so pin the version. **SSD-1B** (Apache-2.0) is a fast fallback at SDXL resolution. Avoid FLUX.1 [dev] not because outputs are unsaleable but because running it commercially needs a paid BFL licence. Avoid SD 3.5 and SDXL-Turbo unless the $1M revenue cap is acceptable and the live licence tag is re-verified. This confirms the CLAUDE.md "schnell only" rule, with the reasoning corrected.
2. **Ownership:** assume the catalogue has **no enforceable copyright**. Do not build a business model that depends on stopping copying. Where a design matters, add and document real human creative input.
3. **Training-data litigation:** not a meaningful downstream risk after *Getty v Stability*; weights are not infringing copies. Watch the 2026 appeal.
