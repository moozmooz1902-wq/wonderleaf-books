# The grammar of what sells

Written after sampling 334 live Fy! artwork files across 30 collections and
**looking at them one at a time**, weighted towards `bestselling-wall-art` and
`bestselling-canvas-prints`. Fy!'s product JSON serves exactly one image per
product and it is the flat artwork — no frame, no room mockup, no watermark —
so what follows describes the art itself, not a product photo.

This is the deep read. The earlier files say *what* subjects exist and in what
volume. This one says **how a picture that sells is actually built**.

---

## The finding in one line

These catalogues are not a pile of pictures. They are **a small number of
composition grammars, crossed with a small number of mark-making techniques,
crossed with a slot list** — and almost every commercial decision in them is
about making an image read correctly **from across a room** while still
rewarding a close look.

Everything below serves that one goal.

---

## 1. The eleven composition grammars

Each is a different *kind* of picture, not a different subject. This is the
axis the competitor catalogues barely use, and it is free: specifying it costs
nothing and it changes every pixel.

**G1 — The framed view.** A window or terrace occupies the left and right
edges as a proscenium; the viewer is inside looking out. Four depth planes:
interior foreground, railing, middle distance, sky. *Seen in: Paris Art
Nouveau window, Capri terrace.* It sells **a window the room does not have**,
and it carries two subjects at once (a still life AND a landscape), which is
why it holds attention.

**G2 — Pure terrain.** No figures, no buildings, no text. Horizon pushed to the
top 8-10% so the land *is* the picture. Either seen across a valley or
**along a ridge in first person**. *Seen in: Snowdonia, Helvellyn.* The
first-person variant sells a memory — "I stood there" — not a view.

**G3 — Hero subject on a decorative habitat.** One creature, upper third,
perched on a pedestal; a dense foreground of seedheads and flowers running off
all four edges; flat banded hills behind. *Seen in: Barn Owl.*

**G4 — Portrait bust on a flat ground.** Subject dead centre, cropped by the
bottom edge, **direct eye contact**, background a single flat colour with no
texture or vignette at all. *Seen in: Budgerigar with flower crown.* The
cheapest grammar to produce and the most swappable.

**G5 — Flat lay on a patterned ground.** Overhead, no horizon, no depth. Two
objects off-centre on a diagonal with air between them. **The pattern does the
composing** — checkerboard, gingham, tile, terrazzo. *Seen in: Bruschetta.*

**G6 — Poster: art panel inside a type margin.** An inset panel with a cream
(never white) margin, and type set in the margin. *Seen in: Cairngorms trail
poster, Chrysanthemum birth flower.* **This is our architecture already.**

**G7 — Specimen chart.** A loose grid of 9-12 subjects on a flat warm ground,
each simplified to a silhouette. *Seen in: Dogs.* Consumes twelve slot values
to make one design, and pairs perfectly with every enumerable list we hold.

**G8 — Minimal object on white.** One object, monochrome ink wash, ~40% of the
frame, vast white space, a line of letterspaced caps beneath. *Seen in: Surf
Trip.*

**G9 — Naive dense cityscape.** Horizontal bands from sky to foreground,
flattened frontal perspective, no vanishing point, decorative flowers and
butterflies floating in the sky. *Seen in: Kitsch Colourful London.*

**G10 — All-over painterly abstraction.** Edge-to-edge marks, no focal point,
no horizon. Reads as a place from a distance and dissolves into marks close up.
*Seen in: Summer Landscape.*

**G11 — Hard-edge geometric.** Circles, arcs, half-discs, a horizon split.
Three flat colours, no texture whatsoever. *Seen in: two mid-century pieces.*

## 2. The three colour strategies

**C1 — A complementary pair carries the image.** Burnt orange against petrol
navy, cobalt against terracotta, vermilion against sage. **Five of the first
eleven images use orange/blue.** This is not coincidence; it is the house trick
for making a print read from across a room, because complementaries hold their
contrast at distance where tonal differences collapse.

**C2 — Warm neutral plus one hot accent.** Cream, bone, oatmeal, sand and
greige across 90-95% of the surface, with rust-orange or coral at roughly 5%.
*Barn owl, Dogs.* This is the palette that makes a mixed catalogue look like
one shop, and it is why Fy!'s wall of thumbnails looks coherent despite the
media underneath being wildly different.

**C3 — Full spectrum at uniform saturation.** Every hue present, held together
not by restraint but by **a consistent saturation level and one dominant
ground** (a pale blue sky). *Kitsch London.* Reserved for joyful and naive
registers.

A fourth, quieter rule sits underneath all three: **realism increases with
distance.** In Snowdonia the foreground is coral and hot pink while the far
peaks are naturalistic blue-grey. The fantasy is in the near field; the
distance stays honest. That gradient is what keeps an invented palette feeling
like a real place.

## 3. The mark-making, and what each technique actually is

Naming the style is useless to a diffusion model. Naming the **physical
artefact** is what works, because the artefact is what the model has seen.

| technique | the artefact that defines it |
|---|---|
| Contour hatching | flat facets filled with dense parallel lines **running along the slope**; direction does the modelling; no tonal shading, no blending |
| Palette knife | angular facets with **knife-straight hard edges**; one flat colour each; strokes running with the form; **no outlines at all** |
| Art Nouveau gouache | flat fills where **every form carries a dark contour line**; foliage as clustered leaf-shapes, never individual leaves |
| Fauvist gouache | unmixed colour, separate visible strokes, and **coloured shadows** — magenta and violet, not grey. Shadow as hue, not darkness |
| Wet-on-wet watercolour | soft bleeds and blooms, **pigment pooling at the edge of a wash**, atmospheric recession, and **the water reserved as bare paper** rather than painted |
| Linocut | pure two-tone, no half-tones anywhere, and **the white lines are the gouge marks**; chatter and speckle from imperfect inking |
| Cut-paper collage | flat torn-edge bands; visible paper tooth; layered planes |
| Oil impasto | loaded brush with **visible ridges and drag**; deliberately uneven square edges; colour shifting within a single stroke |
| Crayon / soft pastel | visible grain, scribbly open fill, childlike simplification |
| Ink wash | grey-black washes with hard dry edges, deliberate splatter dots, unfinished passages |
| Photochrom | hand-tinted monochrome photograph: limited faded palette, grain, dust specks, washed highlights, aged cream border |

## 4. Five rules that hold across almost every image

1. **No gradients.** Anywhere. The only soft transitions in the entire sample
   are deliberate watercolour bleeds. Everything else is flat fill, contour
   line, or hatching. A smooth airbrushed gradient is the single clearest
   signal of cheap generated work, and none of these have one.
2. **The detail gradient.** The subject is the only sharp thing; the
   environment stays flat. The owl has herringbone feather barring while its
   entire habitat is flat bands. The tomatoes are tighter than the
   checkerboard. **This is what makes a subject read instantly at thumbnail
   size**, which is the size at which the buying decision is actually made.
3. **Line goes over mass, never under.** The conifers are inked on top of the
   dry wash; the water ripples are white squiggles over the paint. Draw the
   mass, then draw on it.
4. **Nothing is photographic** — except where photography itself is the
   stylistic quotation (the photochrom). Every mark is a made mark.
5. **Subjects are bottom-anchored and cropped by the lower edge.** The
   budgerigar, the arc fan, the owl's meadow. Nothing floats in the middle of
   the frame.

## 5. The discovery that changes our costs

**A large block of this market should not be generated with a diffusion model
at all. It should be drawn with code.**

The hard-edge geometric work — two circles, a fan of concentric arcs, a
horizon split, a column of half-discs, three flat colours — is **pure
parameterised vector geometry**. A short script with parameters for palette,
arc count, disc count, split height and symmetry produces these perfectly,
instantly, free, and in literally unlimited variation. Asking FLUX for them
would be slower, more expensive, and *worse* — wobbly circles, imperfect
symmetry, colour drift.

**Abstract and geometric is 9.12% of the competitor corpus: 122,958 listings.**
Moving that block to a procedural renderer takes it off the GPU bill entirely
and removes every quality risk from it.

So the catalogue splits into two production lines:

- **Line A, procedural/vector** — hard-edge geometric, mid-century arcs and
  arches, Bauhaus, colour-field, stripes, checkerboards, grids, moon phases,
  concentric forms, and all the type-led products. Cost: effectively zero.
- **Line B, diffusion** — everything painterly, textured or representational:
  terrain, wildlife, botanical, food, framed views, naive cityscapes,
  photochrom.

## 6. What this means for the UK plan, concretely

**Naive style is the hedge for place art.** `Kitsch Colourful London` works
precisely because naive flattening means **architectural accuracy is not
required** — distortion *is* the style. For the hundreds of UK towns where we
have no reference accuracy, the naive and folk registers turn FLUX's weakest
area into a non-issue.

**Photochrom is the second hedge, and it is a gift for Britain.** The faded,
grainy, hand-tinted postcard look hides generative artefacts behind exactly
the defects it is meant to have, and Britain has an enormous Edwardian seaside
postcard heritage to quote: Whitby, Scarborough, Llandudno, Tenby, Brighton,
and the 59 surviving piers and 85 lighthouses in the gazetteer.

**The specimen chart is the most efficient use of our enumerable lists.** One
sheet of twelve consumes twelve slot values and produces one highly saleable
design — and the grouping itself is an axis: by family, by county, by habitat,
by colour. 636 British birds, 221 dog breeds, 59 butterflies, native livestock,
fungi, trees, shells.

**The birth-flower scaffold is the best template in the sample for us**, because
the *generated area is the smallest of any image seen* and everything else is
type, which our renderer already sets perfectly. `{index} {month} / {meanings}
/ {panel} / {NAME}` transposes far beyond flowers — birthstones, saints' days,
**county flowers**, breeds, constellations, lighthouses.

**And the simplest, highest-volume template remains the portrait bust on a flat
ground.** Fixed composition, fixed lighting, flat single-colour background,
swap the species. Their version runs to 96 species and leans American. The
British List alone has 636.

## 7. What we must not copy

- **Signatures.** Three of the sampled images carry a painted artist signature.
  Fy! sells artist work; we sell generated work. The OCR gate exists to catch
  this, and it is now a confirmed necessity rather than a precaution.
- **Recognisable branded objects.** The `Surf Trip` ink wash is unmistakably a
  Porsche 911. Car models, and anything with a registered shape, stay out.
- **Displate's recent register.** Its 2024-26 work is converging on
  AI-photoreal hyper-saturation — glossy, smooth, gradient-heavy. That is
  exactly the look rule 1 says to avoid, and exactly where near-duplication
  lives.

---

## 8. Honest limits

- This is **one retailer studied closely** (Fy!) plus earlier Displate
  sampling. Both sample sets are modest and neither site publishes sales data,
  so "bestselling" means *the collection they label bestselling*, which is a
  merchandising choice and not a measurement.
- Eleven images are described in depth here out of 334 downloaded. The
  grammars and rules are drawn from those eleven plus the earlier 156-image
  pass; frequencies such as "five of eleven use orange/blue" are indicative of
  a strong tendency, not a measured rate across the catalogue.
- Several sampled images are themselves probably AI-generated. They are
  evidence of **what this market accepts and sells**, which is what we need,
  but not of a craft tradition.
