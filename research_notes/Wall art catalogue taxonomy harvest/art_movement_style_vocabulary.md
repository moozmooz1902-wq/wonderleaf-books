# Art-Historical and Stylistic Vocabulary as a Copyright-Safe Style Axis

Scope note for the report writer: this is a vocabulary harvest, not a market analysis. Where a
date range or a visual-characteristic list appears without an inline URL it is a compression of
standard art-historical description for prompt use, drawn from the movement names harvested from
the glossaries cited below — it is flagged as such in each section's Inferences block. Every death
year used for the public-domain cut carries an explicit verification status.

Verification key used throughout:
- **[V2]** — death year confirmed in this session's searches, with the search returning two or more
  corroborating sources (e.g. Wikipedia plus a museum/gallery/institutional page).
- **[V1]** — death year confirmed in this session's searches from effectively one source.
- **[M]** — from model knowledge only, NOT re-verified this session. Usable where the margin to the
  1955 cut is wide (death before ~1945); **must be verified before use** where it is within ten
  years of the cut.
- **[UNVERIFIED — DO NOT USE]** — cannot be relied on for the PD cut.

## What art movements and periods do museum print shops and poster retailers organise their catalogues by?

### Takeaway

Museum print shops merchandise by a surprisingly short list of movements — typically six to twelve
"featured movements" plus centuries, cultures, subjects, colour and room — while the museums'
*scholarly* glossaries (Tate 461 terms, MoMA's terms index) carry the long tail of several hundred
movement and technique names. The commercial browse trees are far shallower than the art-historical
vocabulary available, which means the long tail is an unexploited naming axis.

### Inferences

- The commercially merchandised movement set is small and repeats across institutions. The
  intersection of the Met and V&A featured lists gives the **core commercial movement vocabulary**:
  Art Deco, Arts & Crafts, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Romanticism, Realism, Neoclassical,
  Modernism, Decorative Arts, Japanese/East Asian, Harlem Renaissance, Vintage Posters.
- Non-movement axes dominate retail browse trees (subject, colour, room, artist A–Z). Any generated
  catalogue should carry **movement, subject, colour and room as independent tags** because that is
  how the trade indexes stock.
- The Tate and MoMA glossaries, not the shops, are the right harvest source for a long style axis:
  461 and several hundred named terms respectively versus roughly a dozen shop facets.

### Gaps

- **Tate shop prints landing page returned a 404 body** behind the navigation, so the shop's "Prints
  by theme" and "Prints by colour" enumerated *values* were not captured — only the facet names.
- **Royal Academy, British Museum, Rijksmuseum, Art Institute of Chicago, MoMA Design Store and
  Smithsonian browse trees were not captured.** Rijksmuseum art-movements page returned 404; Art
  Institute collection and open-access pages returned 403; British Museum shop prints returned 404.
  Their movement facets remain unharvested and should be re-attempted with different URL paths.
- Tate's and MoMA's full glossary indexes are paginated; only the first index page of each was
  retrieved, so roughly 430 Tate terms and the D–Z of MoMA's index are unharvested.

## Which movements are safe to imitate by name under the UK public-domain cut?

### Takeaway

Under UK law copyright in an artistic work expires 70 years from the end of the calendar year of the
author's death, so in 2026 the clean cut is **died 1955 or earlier**; artists who died in 1956 come
into the public domain on 1 January 2027. Fourteen movements clear the cut on every principal artist
checked; six are blocked by a single late-dying member; Bauhaus, Art Deco (as a designer-led style)
and everything post-1940 do not clear.

### Cited Findings

- UK statute: "Copyright in artistic works expires at the end of the period of 70 years from the end
  of the calendar year in which the author dies" — [Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 s.12, legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12)
- For works of unknown authorship, the term runs 70 years from the end of the calendar year the work
  was made, or from first making available to the public if that happens in that period — [CDPA 1988 s.12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12)
- For works of joint authorship the term runs from the death of the last surviving author — [CDPA 1988 s.12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12)
- Computer-generated works: 50 years from the end of the calendar year the work was made — [CDPA 1988 s.12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12)
- Corroborating plain-English statements of the UK term: [Art UK, Copyright explained](https://artuk.org/about/copyright-explained); [University of Nottingham, Duration of copyright](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/library/help/copyright/basics/duration.aspx); [CopyrightUser.org, How long does copyright last?](https://copyrightuser.org/?p=3009)

### Inferences

- Technique tokens are the only part of this vocabulary with **zero copyright surface**: a process
  name cannot be owned, and there is no artist death year to check. For a catalogue that must avoid
  near-duplication, technique is also the highest-variance axis — the same subject rendered as
  mezzotint, risograph, cyanotype and sumi-e produces four genuinely different images, whereas the
  same subject across four Impressionist painters produces four near-duplicates.
- Museum glossaries already mix technique and movement in one flat index (MoMA's A-list runs
  "Abstract Expressionism / Abstraction / Acrylic paint / Action painting"), which supports treating
  technique and movement as **two orthogonal tags on the same item** rather than one "style" field.

### Gaps

- No single authoritative glossary covering all 65 technique names was retrieved; the Tate and MoMA
  glossary pages confirm roughly 25 of them by name, and the remainder are standard printmaking and
  illustration terminology compressed here into prompt language without a per-term citation.
- "Risograph", "duotone", "gradient mesh", "pixel", "ASCII" and "blackletter" were not found in the
  museum glossaries retrieved; they are current-practice terms rather than museum-catalogued media.

### Inferences

- The 2026 interior vocabulary polarises into two usable prompt clusters: a **calm/restraint cluster**

  above, which means a catalogue should carry both rather than betting on one.
- Interior labels are a palette-and-texture axis, technique is a surface axis, and movement is a
  draughtsmanship axis. They combine multiplicatively, which is the structural answer to
